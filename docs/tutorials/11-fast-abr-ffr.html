Fast acquisition: ABR and FFR

Auditory brainstem responses (ABR) and frequency-following responses (FFR) last a few milliseconds and contain frequencies of hundreds to thousands of hertz: the Portiloop's usual 250 Hz cannot record them. This tutorial explains the fast acquisition of the Portiloop v2.3 (2 to 16 kSPS), its two auditory pipelines, "Click ABR (fast)" and "FFR (fast)", the files they write, and how to turn those files into averaged responses.

Why a fast acquisition

The two responses are short and fast:

  • ABR. After a click, the scalp records a series of small waves from the auditory nerve and the brainstem, numbered with Roman numerals since Jewett and Williston described them within the first 10 ms after the click [2]. They are sub-microvolt and sharp. Skoe and Kraus, describing the filters used for brainstem responses to complex sounds, write that "the bandpass filters match the range of settings used for click-ABRs and typically fall in the range of 100-3000 Hz" [3].
  • FFR. To a periodic sound such as a vowel, neurons of the auditory pathway phase-lock to the waveform, and the scalp potential follows the sound's periodicity (its fundamental frequency, F0) and part of its harmonics: "Typically all harmonics present in the stimulus are present in the FFR, at least up to about 1.2-1.3 kHz" [4]. Analyses are typically filtered "at or around 70-2000 Hz" [4].

The overnight pipelines record at 250 Hz: one sample every 4 ms, so two or three samples per ABR. The sampling rate is not the only limit: the ADS1299 amplifier of the Portiloop has a digital filter whose bandwidth follows the data rate. Its datasheet lists the -3 dB bandwidth at each rate [6]:

At 250 Hz the amplifier itself removes everything above about 65 Hz, before any software sees the signal: an ABR or an FFR at 98 Hz is not there to be recovered. The filter is "a third-order sinc filter" which, after a step change at the input, "takes 3 × tDR to settle" [6] (tDR is the sample period). A sinc filter is symmetric, so its delay is half of that, 1.5 sample periods: this is our calculation, not a datasheet value. It is a constant delay that the trigger times do not include (see Triggers). For an ABR, 8 or 16 kSPS keep the 100 to 3000 Hz band; for an FFR analysed up to 2000 Hz, 8 kSPS is at the limit (2096 Hz at -3 dB) and 16 kSPS keeps it. The code does not recommend a rate; its default for the fast pipelines is 8000 (DEFAULT_FAST_FREQUENCY = 8000, simple_gui.py, line 55), and the pull request's ABR test was made at 16 kSPS.

Averaging N trials divides the random noise by √N: from 5 µV to 0.16 µV with 1,000 trials and 0.05 µV with 10,000, which is why ABR runs have thousands of clicks. Averaging cannot bring back what the amplifier's filter has removed: at 250 Hz the average converges to a slow, delayed bump, not to an ABR.

How the fast acquisition works

The README of the fast acquisition explains the problem in one sentence: "Python cannot read the ADS1299 faster than about 1 kSPS, so the capture runs in a C program, adscapture.c, which reads each conversion by driving the SPI controller's registers directly through a small kernel module, spimap." (README.md, lines 3 to 6). In more detail (README lines 58 to 72; adscapture.c, header):

  • The amplifier is configured through the normal kernel driver: reset, the same register settings as the other pipelines (mod_config()), read back and checked, then START.
  • Each conversion is then read by the program itself, without the kernel: about 18 µs per read at 16 MHz, against 65 to 130 µs through the kernel driver. The "data ready" line of the ADS1299 (DRDY) is polled on CPU 3 by a thread with real-time priority (SCHED_FIFO, priority 10).
  • A writer thread on the other CPUs writes the CSV, through a buffer of 524,288 conversions ("32 s at 16 kSPS"), so that a slow SD card does not delay the reads. It also sends a decimated copy (means of blocks of samples, 250 Hz) to the Python process, which feeds the Control Center's display and, if "Stream LSL" is checked, the LSL stream "Portiloop Raw Data" (fast_capture.py, lines 158 to 163). The README: "It is only meant for viewing."
  • The program stops on the GUI's Stop, at the end of the duration (or of the run, for the ABR and FFR pipelines), when less than 200 MB are free, if DRDY stops for 1 s, or if its parent process dies.

What the fast acquisition does not do

"Not available in this pipeline: filtering, detection, stimulation, and lead-off detection during the recording (its checks need register commands, which would interrupt the reads). The electrodes OFF at the last impedance check are disabled, as in the other pipelines."README.md, lines 54 to 56 [1]

In practice: the CSV contains the raw signals only (the notch setting does not apply); an electrode that comes off during the run is not detected or removed from the bias loop as it is during a night (tutorial 04), so check the impedances just before each run; and the "Stimulation starts after" delay of the Advanced tab is not used: start_fast_capture() receives the pause flag but never reads it (lines 97 to 104), so the sounds of an ABR or FFR run start about 1 s after the capture starts, whatever that setting says.

Lost samples

The program keeps one CSV row per conversion even when a read fails: "A missed conversion, a frame with a bad status byte, or one lost because the writer was too slow, repeats the previous row, and its row index is listed in lost_samples of _stats.json, as in the other pipelines." (README lines 40 to 43). The README's tests with the amplifier's internal test signal:

Installation on the Portiloop

The fast pipelines appear in the Pipeline list only on a Portiloop where they were installed: custom_pipelines.py adds "Acquisition only (fast)", "FFR (fast)" and "Click ABR (fast)" only if the capture program is built, /dev/spimap exists and is accessible, and the SPI controller is kept powered (fast_capture_status(); custom_pipelines.py, lines 39 to 78).

  1. Put the code under review on the device. The Portiloop runs its software from ~/portiloop-software. Check out branch fast-acquisition (or the pull request's head) there, with Git LFS, since the sound files are stored with it. How you update a device depends on how it was set up; this is not documented in the branch.
  2. Run the installer, as the user mendel (not with sudo): 
    bash ~/portiloop-software/portiloop/fast_capture/install.sh
sudo systemctl restart simplegui
    The script (install.sh) refuses to run unless the kernel is 4.19.125-mtk and the prebuilt module's "vermagic" matches the kernel's own modules; it compiles adscapture and the sound player ffrplay with gcc ("libasound2-dev is needed" for the player), installs spimap.ko under /lib/modules/4.19.125-mtk/extra, loads it at boot, and installs a udev rule that gives /dev/spimap to the group input and keeps the SPI controller powered.
  3. Check. After the restart, the Pipeline list of the Advanced tab (tutorial 03) shows the three fast pipelines, and the Sample frequency list offers 2000, 4000, 8000 and 16000 for them.
Another kernel needs another moduleThe module was built "on red-ibis for the Mendel kernel 4.19.125-mtk" (README line 23). For any other kernel it must be rebuilt on the Portiloop itself; the README gives the steps (lines 167 to 208), which need the Mendel kernel source, not included in the repository. The module cannot be unloaded: to remove it, delete the three installed files, run sudo depmod -a and reboot (lines 27 to 28).
Security note from the README"Any process that can open /dev/spimap (root and the group input) can drive the SPI controller, including its DMA addresses, which could be used to read or write the physical memory. On the Portiloops, mendel is in that group and already has sudo." (lines 232 to 236).

Running a recording

  1. Electrodes and impedance. Place the electrodes (see Montage) and run the Check tab. An electrode OFF at that check is switched off in the recording (tutorial 03); there is no lead-off check during the run.
  2. Advanced tab. Pipeline "Click ABR (fast)" or "FFR (fast)"; Sample frequency 8000 or 16000 (see above); Sound output volume as calibrated; "Save recording locally" checked; Cond B to play the sounds, Cond A for a silent run with triggers (below).
  3. Test Sound plays a few seconds of the run's stimuli at the current volume (below). It is refused during a run: "No test sound during a run." (simple_gui.py, lines 484 to 497).
  4. Start. One Start is one run: the capture starts, then the player; after 1 s of silence (longer if the sound card is still held by a previous sound, see Triggers) the stimuli begin. The participant should be still and relaxed (an ABR is easily buried in muscle activity); whether awake or asleep is a choice of your protocol.
  5. End. The recording stops by itself at the end of the run (the log says "End of the FFR run: stopping the capture." or "End of the Click ABR run: ...", fast_capture.py, lines 202 to 205): about 91.5 s for a Click ABR run, about 201.4 s for an FFR run (our computation from the parameters, see below). Stop ends it earlier. The LED is cyan during a Cond B run, purple during a Cond A run and yellow at the end (leds.led1(...), lines 113 and 272).

Montage

The code and the README of the fast acquisition do not say where to put the electrodes, and the lab's protocol for ABR and FFR recordings is not documented in the repository (the FFR code refers to a protocol document, "FFR_Timeline_and_parameters.pdf", which is not in it). We can only say what is common and how it maps onto the Portiloop.

"For cABRs, a vertical one-channel montage is the most common configuration." Their preferred placements: Cz (active), "ipsilateral earlobe (reference), and forehead or contralateral earlobe (ground)."Skoe and Kraus (2010), a tutorial on brainstem responses to complex sounds [3]

On the Portiloop, every channel is an electrode minus the REF electrode, and the BIAS electrode is the driven ground (tutorial 02). The CLAS montage already contains such a vertical derivation: channel 6 is Cz, recorded against the REF on the right mastoid, with the bias on the right earlobe; channels 7 (Fz) and 8 (Fpz) are midline-to-mastoid derivations too. The Portiloop plays "the same sound in both ears" (CHANNELS = 2, ffr.py, line 77), so "ipsilateral" has no meaning here. Choose the montage with whoever designed your study and record it in your notes: the files do not store electrode names.

Earphones and stimulus artifactSkoe and Kraus recommend that "Stimulus artifact can be minimized by using electromagnetically shielded insert earphones", warn that "Because circumaural headphones increase the chances for stimulus artifact contamination" and that, if the transducer is not properly shielded, "the electrical signal can 'leak' and get picked up by the electrodes" [3]. The CLAS study uses small in-ear earphones taped to the ears (tutorial 04); the pull request does not say which earphones its ABR test used. Unshielded earphones close to mastoid electrodes are a likely source of artifact; the polarity alternation (below) cancels it in the added average but not in the subtracted one.

Why the timing is random

The first version of the pipeline (8cbbfb3) used a fixed SOA of 32 ms ("1536 frames, 31.25 Hz"). The second commit replaced it, and the README explains why:

"Why the jitter: with a fixed SOA of 32 ms (1.5 audio periods of 1024 frames), the periodic activity of the Portiloop was time-locked to the clicks and left two artifacts in the average (about 1 and 11.7 ms after the trigger, after the + and the - clicks). A random SOA spreads it over the epoch, as the line noise."README.md, lines 142 to 144 [1]

The "audio period" is the block of 1024 frames in which the sound system hands samples to the sound card (period_size 1024 at rate 48000 in the device's asound.conf): 21.33 ms. With a fixed 32 ms SOA, two clicks span exactly 3 periods, so every + click falls at the same place in the period (frame 896 of 1024, by our computation from the code's onsets) and every - click half a period later (frame 384). Anything the device does once per period then sits at a fixed latency after the + clicks and at a latency 10.67 ms different after the - clicks. Our arithmetic: 1 ms + 10.67 ms = 11.67 ms, which is consistent with the two artifacts the authors saw, if one event per audio period caused both. With the random SOA, the positions of the clicks in the period are spread evenly: we checked it on 200 runs built with the code (each of 16 bins of the period holds between 0.98 and 1.02 of its expected share, for both polarities), which agrees with the pull request's own check. The widget shows the effect.

The FFR pipeline

"FFR (fast)" (ffr.py, branch fast-acquisition, changed by the pull request) plays the syllable /da/ in four conditions during a fast acquisition.

The /da/. One file, portiloop/sounds/ffr/da_positive.wav: mono, 32-bit float, 44.1 kHz, 5292 samples (120 ms), RMS 0.100 and peak 0.334 of full scale. Its description file (da_positive.json) says how it was made: "Praat KlattGrid; tmda-pav CV presets v3 (filtered stop release)", consonant "d", vowel "a", "frequency_hz": 98.0, "consonant_duration_ms": 20.0, 2 ms onset and offset ramps. It is therefore a synthetic /da/ with an F0 of 98 Hz (our autocorrelation of the file also gives 98.0 Hz), not the 40 ms or 170 ms /da/ used in many FFR studies [4]. When the run is prepared, /da/ is resampled once to the 48 kHz of the sound output with scipy.signal.resample_poly (160/147), giving 5760 frames; the negative /da/ is "exactly -1 x the positive /da/" (make_conditions(), render()).

ConditionCodeSound
+1positive /da/
-2negative /da/ (-1 × /da/)
+N3positive /da/ + a new pink-noise token
-N4negative /da/ + a new pink-noise token

CONDITIONS = (("+", 1), ("-", 2), ("+N", 3), ("-N", 4)), ffr.py, lines 64 to 70. The noise is added, not inverted, in -N.

  • Noise (changed by the pull request): each noise trial gets its own pink noise, generated at 48 kHz by make_noise() (lines 118 to 139): white Gaussian noise whose spectrum is shaped to 1/f power between 20 Hz and 22.05 kHz, 120 ms long, 2 ms raised-cosine ramps, scaled to "+10 dB SNR against /da/ (RMS over the 120 ms)". So the noise RMS is 0.0316 of full scale, against 0.100 for /da/. Each token comes from its own seed (noise_seed in _trials.csv), so it can be regenerated: "No token is ever repeated, so the noise does not average into the FFR." The first version used four fixed files, noise1.wav to noise4.wav, which are no longer used.
  • Blocks: 250 blocks of the four conditions, shuffled within each block (rng.permutation(4)): 1000 trials, 250 per condition; "a condition can repeat back to back across blocks".
  • Timing: SOA uniformly random between 195 and 205 ms, rounded to the frame, so 75 to 85 ms of silence between the end of one /da/ and the next; 1 s of silence before the first trial, 0.5 s after the last. A run lasts about 201.4 s (we built five runs with the code: 201.37 to 201.55 s; the docstring says "about 3.3 min", the README "about 3.4 min"). "A session is 4 runs, so 4 recordings."
  • Level: /da/ has the same level in all conditions, "the level calibrated at 80 dB SPL" (docstring, line 13). The loudest sample of a run is "about 0.46 of full scale" according to the pull request; in our five runs it was 0.440 to 0.453. Each trial is checked for clipping before the run starts.

Test Sound and Cond A

  • Test Sound, with "FFR (fast)" selected: "a train of positive /da/ at 200 ms" for 5 s, 25 tokens (test_sound(), ffr.py, lines 412 to 439). The README says to use it to "calibrate it for 80 dB SPL" (a sound level meter and a coupler for your earphones are needed; the procedure is not described). With "Click ABR (fast)": "the clicks of a run (alternating, jittered SOA)" for 5 s, 111 clicks (click_abr.py, lines 180 to 208). Test Sound plays outside the recording and writes nothing.
  • Cond A: "With the stimulation off ("Cond A"), the run is the same, with triggers, in silence." The player still runs and plays silence, and the trigger codes are written at the times the stimuli would have been played. The file name ends in _CONDA.
A use for Cond A (our suggestion)Averaging a Cond A run on its triggers shows anything locked to the trials that is not caused by the sound reaching the ear: activity of the device or the player, or an artifact of the trigger timing. It should be flat. It does not test the electrical leak of the earphones (no sound is played); for that, a run with the earphones playing but not in the ears, or their tube clamped for insert earphones, is the usual check.

Triggers: how a code gets into the right row

The sound card and the amplifier have their own clocks, and the device's operating system cannot play a sound at an exact moment. The code does not try to; it measures when each onset is played and puts the code in the row recorded at that time (README lines 101 to 110; ffrplay.c, header; adscapture.c, lines 263 to 334):

  1. One sound for the whole run. The Python side renders the run (silences included) as one 48 kHz stream, so that the SOAs are "exact to the frame (1/48 ms)", and writes the schedule: trial, code and onset frame of each stimulus.
  2. The player follows the sound card's clock. ffrplay writes the stream in chunks of 5 ms through the softvol device (so the volume of the Control Center applies), and after each write asks ALSA which frame is being played, with the time (CLOCK_MONOTONIC). It fits a straight line to the last 2 s of these pairs and, when it writes an onset, sends "<trial> <code> <time>" to adscapture, about 100 ms before the onset is played.
  3. The capture program follows the amplifier's clock. Each row has an expected time, from a reference that follows the DRDY reads and whose period is re-estimated every second. The code of an onset goes into the row whose time is nearest; offset_us is the remaining difference (at most half a sample period: 31 µs at 16 kSPS).
  4. Problems are counted. A trigger that arrives after its row was written is "late": it is listed with its row in _triggers.csv and _trials.csv but not written in the CSV; two triggers in one row keep the first ("same_row"). The counts are in _stats.json, and the Python side logs "some triggers may be wrong" if there was an underrun, a late trigger or a shared row.

The authors' measurements, quoted from the README (lines 112 to 122, "On bored-yarn (18 Nov 2026)"): the onset times "lie on a straight line in the onset frames to within 2 to 3 us RMS (14 us at most)"; "Over a run, the ADS's clock moves by up to 2.6 ms against the Coral's (0.25 % slower than nominal, varying by a few ppm): counting samples from the first trigger would put the last ones up to 40 rows off at 16 kSPS"; runs of 1000 trials at 8 and 16 kSPS had "no underrun, all the triggers in the CSV, 9 missed conversions (filled) in the run of 1000 trials at 16 kSPS, none at 8 kSPS".

What the trigger time does not include"Not included in the onset time: the latency of the codec, amplifier and earphones after the audio DMA, and the delay of the ADS1299's filter. Both are constant; measure them once (for example, the sound output through a divider into a channel) to place the onsets in absolute time." (README lines 124 to 126). Neither delay has been measured in the repository, and we have not measured them. Add to them the travel time of sound from the transducer to the eardrum (in air, about 1 ms for 34 cm; for insert earphones, the length of their tube). Until they are measured, ABR and FFR latencies from the Portiloop are relative: fine for comparing conditions or sessions recorded with the same setup, not for comparing with published normative latencies. A latency correction then shifts every trigger by the same number of samples.

One more detail: the sound system's PulseAudio daemon "keeps the sound card for about 5 s after a sound played through it", and the player waits for it (up to 10 s, OPEN_TIMEOUT), so a run started right after Test Sound has a longer silence before its first trial. The triggers are not affected.

The files

On the hard drive: one ABR or FFR runSame folder and names as the other pipelines (tutorial 05): <name>_<date>_<time>_CONDB/ on the SD card (or in the home folder of the internal memory without a card). Inside:
  • <name>.csv: raw_ch1 to raw_ch8 in µV with 3 decimals ("the LSB is 0.022 uV at gain 24"), then trigger: the code (1 to 4 for the FFR, 5 or 6 for the ABR) in the row of each onset, 0 elsewhere. ("Acquisition only (fast)" writes stimulation_on, always 0, instead.) A new file <name>_part2.csv, ... starts every 28,800,000 rows, which a single run never reaches (30 minutes at 16 kSPS).
  • <name>_trials.csv: one line per trial (the design), completed after the recording with the row of its trigger.
  • <name>_triggers.csv: the triggers as written by the capture program: trial,code,row,onset_time,offset_us,in_csv.
  • <name>_metadata.json: the settings, with a fast_capture section and an ffr or click_abr section.
  • <name>_stats.json: the sampling statistics, with a fast_capture section and an ffr_playback or click_abr_playback section.

_metadata.json and _stats.json

Analysis

The repository has no analysis code for these recordings; portiloop_analysis (tutorials 06 to 09) is written for 250 Hz nights. The usual steps, which the sketch below implements:

  1. Load the channel of the active electrode (all CSV parts, in order), the rate from the metadata, the trials with their rows, and the lost rows.
  2. Band-pass the continuous signal: 100 to 3000 Hz for the ABR [3], 70 to 2000 Hz for the FFR [4], with a zero-phase filter (forward and backward, so that latencies are not shifted), and a notch at the mains frequency (50 or 60 Hz) if mains noise is strong. The upper edge must stay below half the sampling rate: at 2 kSPS, the ABR band cannot be used as such.
  3. Epochs around each trigger row: -5 to +15 ms for the ABR (the next click comes at least 40 ms later), -40 to +200 ms for the FFR; subtract the mean of the pre-stimulus samples; drop epochs with a lost row or with an amplitude above a threshold (the 40 µV in the sketch is a placeholder: choose yours on your data).
  4. Average per polarity, then add and subtract.
  5. Reliability: correlate two independent halves.
  6. Latencies: correct for the constant delays if they have been measured (see Triggers).

Adding and subtracting the two polarities

Anything that follows the sign of the stimulus (the electrical leak of the earphones, the cochlear microphonic of the hair cells) is inverted between the two polarities; anything that does not is the same in both. So:

  • ABR: the added average (+ and - averaged together) cancels the stimulus artifact and the cochlear microphonic: in Skoe and Kraus's words, "stimulus artifact and CM can be minimized from the response by adding responses to alternating stimulus polarities" [3]. The subtracted average shows what was cancelled, which is useful to see how large the artifact was.
  • FFR: "Adding the responses to da0 and daπ biases the FFR to the envelope (FFRENV)", and "subtracting the responses to da0 and daπ biases the FFR to the fine structure" (TFS) [4]; Aiken and Picton separated envelope and spectral responses to vowels in the same way [5]. The envelope response is dominated by F0 (98 Hz here); the fine-structure response by the harmonics, and it keeps any stimulus artifact, which the addition cancels. Compute these from codes 1 and 2 (quiet) and, separately, from codes 3 and 4 (in noise).
Adding and subtracting polarities, with the real /da/ of the FFR pipeline

What to try: look at the two sounds; then at the modelled responses; then at their sum and difference, with and without the stimulus artifact. The sound is the real da_positive.wav (resampled to 16 kHz). The responses are a model, not data: each polarity's response is the half-wave rectified sound (a crude stand-in for auditory nerve fibres firing during one phase of the pressure wave), low-pass filtered at 1 kHz and delayed by 8 ms (cABRs occur "at approximately 6-10 ms post-stimulus onset" [3]); the artifact is a scaled copy of the sound with no delay. Everything is band-passed 70 to 2000 Hz, as in the analysis.

Split-half reliability, and a trap of strict alternation

A common check of an averaged response is to split the trials in two halves, average each, and correlate the two averages over the response window; "One method is to average the odd sweeps separate from the even sweeps" [4]. The pull request reports such a check for the ABR: "In the averaged ABR (10 000 trials), wave V is clear and the odd/even halves agree (r = 0.97)."

Odd and even trials are the two polaritiesThe clicks alternate strictly, starting with +: every odd trial is a + click and every even trial a - click. Splitting by odd and even trial numbers therefore compares the + average with the - average, not two equivalent halves: it mixes reliability with the polarity-dependent parts (artifact, cochlear microphonic). Split by pairs instead (trials 1-2, 5-6, ... against 3-4, 7-8, ...), so that each half has both polarities, and compare the added averages of the halves. The pull request does not say how its halves were made. (Two thousand clicks per run also means that 10,000 trials are five runs; the pull request does not say how the runs were combined.)

A Python sketch

A minimal analysis with pandas, NumPy and SciPy. Replace the two prefixes with the path of a recording without .csv (for example /media/sd/abr_2026-11-20_10-00-00_CONDB/abr_2026-11-20_10-00-00_CONDB) and set channel and mains for your montage and site.

import glob, json, re
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy.signal import butter, filtfilt, iirnotch, sosfiltfilt


def load_run(prefix, channel="raw_ch6"):
    """One fast recording: the channel in uV (all CSV parts), its rate, the trials and the lost rows."""
    meta = json.load(open(prefix + "_metadata.json"))
    stats = json.load(open(prefix + "_stats.json"))
    parts = sorted(glob.glob(prefix + "_part*.csv"), key=lambda p: int(re.search(r"_part(\d+)\.csv$", p).group(1)))
    x = np.concatenate([pd.read_csv(p, usecols=[channel])[channel].to_numpy(float)
                        for p in [prefix + ".csv"] + parts])
    trials = pd.read_csv(prefix + "_trials.csv")
    trials = trials[trials["row"].notna()].copy()      # a trial without a row was not played or not received
    trials["row"] = trials["row"].astype(int)
    return x, meta["frequency"], trials, np.array(stats["lost_samples"], dtype=int)


def epochs(x, fs, trials, lost, band, tmin, tmax, reject_uv, mains=None):
    """Band-pass (zero phase), optional mains notch, epochs around each trigger row, baseline, rejection."""
    xf = sosfiltfilt(butter(2, band, btype="bandpass", fs=fs, output="sos"), x)
    if mains:                                          # 50 or 60 (Hz): the mains frequency of your site
        xf = filtfilt(*iirnotch(mains, 30, fs), xf)
    lags = np.arange(int(round(tmin * fs)), int(round(tmax * fs)) + 1)
    ok = (trials["row"] + lags[0] >= 0) & (trials["row"] + lags[-1] < len(x))
    trials = trials[ok]
    idx = trials["row"].to_numpy()[:, None] + lags
    ep = xf[idx]
    ep -= ep[:, lags < 0].mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)  # baseline: the samples before the trigger
    bad = np.isin(idx, lost).any(axis=1) | (np.abs(ep).max(axis=1) > reject_uv)
    return lags / fs, ep[~bad], trials[~bad].reset_index(drop=True)


def split_half_r(t, ep, trials, codes, window):
    """Correlation of two halves made of alternate PAIRS of trials (each half keeps both polarities)."""
    pair = (trials["trial"] - 1) // 2
    halves = []
    for h in (0, 1):
        sel = (pair % 2 == h).to_numpy()
        halves.append(np.mean([ep[sel & (trials["code"] == c).to_numpy()].mean(axis=0) for c in codes], axis=0))
    w = (t >= window[0]) & (t <= window[1])
    return np.corrcoef(halves[0][w], halves[1][w])[0, 1]


# ---- Click ABR (codes 5 = +, 6 = -): 100 to 3000 Hz, -5 to +15 ms
x, fs, trials, lost = load_run("ABR_RECORDING_PREFIX")          # without ".csv"
t, ep, tr = epochs(x, fs, trials, lost, (100, min(3000, 0.45 * fs)), -0.005, 0.015, reject_uv=40, mains=60)
plus = ep[(tr["code"] == 5).to_numpy()].mean(axis=0)
minus = ep[(tr["code"] == 6).to_numpy()].mean(axis=0)
abr = (plus + minus) / 2            # the ABR: stimulus artifact and cochlear microphonic cancel
artifact = (plus - minus) / 2       # what follows the polarity: stimulus artifact, cochlear microphonic
print(len(tr), "epochs kept of", len(trials), "; split-half r (1 to 10 ms) =",
      round(split_half_r(t, ep, tr, (5, 6), (0.001, 0.010)), 3))

# ---- FFR (codes 1 = +/da/, 2 = -/da/; 3 and 4 with noise): 70 to 2000 Hz, -40 to +200 ms
x, fs, trials, lost = load_run("FFR_RECORDING_PREFIX")
t, ep, tr = epochs(x, fs, trials, lost, (70, min(2000, 0.45 * fs)), -0.040, 0.200, reject_uv=40, mains=60)
r_pos = ep[(tr["code"] == 1).to_numpy()].mean(axis=0)
r_neg = ep[(tr["code"] == 2).to_numpy()].mean(axis=0)
env = (r_pos + r_neg) / 2           # added: envelope-following response (F0)
tfs = (r_pos - r_neg) / 2           # subtracted: temporal fine structure (and any stimulus artifact)
w = (t >= 0.010) & (t < 0.130)      # response window: adjust to your latency
f = np.fft.rfftfreq(w.sum(), 1 / fs)
spec = lambda y: 2 * np.abs(np.fft.rfft(y[w] * np.hanning(w.sum()))) / np.hanning(w.sum()).sum()
print("F0 (98 Hz) amplitude in the envelope response:", spec(env)[np.argmin(np.abs(f - 98))], "uV")

Points for the review

Things we noticed while reading the code under review, for its authors:

  1. The odd/even split-half of the PR's test splits by polarity under strict alternation (above); the method and the way five runs make 10,000 trials should be stated.
  2. "Stimulation starts after" is silently ignored by the fast pipelines (pause_value is never read in start_fast_capture()), while the metadata still records sleep_timeout and the stimulation delays. A note in the GUI or the README, or leaving them out of the metadata, would avoid confusion.
  3. The click's acoustic polarity ("condensation") and level are not documented or measured; the README gives a level only for /da/ (80 dB SPL).
  4. The FFR code and metadata refer to FFR_Timeline_and_parameters.pdf, which is not in the repository; the montage is not documented anywhere.
  5. Run durations differ slightly between texts: FFR "about 3.3 min" (docstring) and "about 3.4 min" (README) for 201.4 s; ABR "about 92 s" (docstring, README) and "~91 s" (PR) for 91.5 s.
  6. The constant delays (codec, earphones, ADS1299 filter) are acknowledged but not measured; absolute latencies depend on them.
  7. da_negative.wav and noise1.wav to noise4.wav are still shipped but unused (the PR says so), and old FFR _trials.csv files have a noise column where new ones have noise_seed.

Exercises

Which rate?

You plan an FFR analysis up to 2000 Hz and a click ABR analysed between 100 and 3000 Hz, in the same session. Which sampling rate do you choose, and why not 4000?

Answer

16 kSPS. The ADS1299 filter is -3 dB at 1048 Hz at 4 kSPS and 2096 Hz at 8 kSPS: at 4 kSPS both bands are cut by the amplifier itself, and at 8 kSPS the FFR band is at the limit and the ABR band (up to 3000 Hz) is attenuated. At 16 kSPS the -3 dB point is 4193 Hz. The cost is the file size: about 110 to 146 MB per ABR run and 242 to 322 MB per FFR run.

Reading the trials

In an FFR _trials.csv, a trial (an invented example) has condition -N, noise_seed 3274565379, row 1,316,977 and in_csv 0. What sound did the participant hear, where is its epoch, and is the code in the CSV?

Answer

The negative /da/ plus the pink-noise token made by ffr.make_noise(3274565379, token_frames, noise_rms) (the two values are in the ffr section of the metadata). Its epoch starts from CSV row 1,316,977 (counting from 0 at the first data row, the pandas index). in_csv 0 means the code 4 is not in the trigger column: the trigger was late or shared its row; the row in _trials.csv is still computed from its time. Check triggers.late in _stats.json, and decide whether to keep such trials.

An artifact at 1 ms

A colleague shows you an added click ABR from the first version of the pipeline (fixed 32 ms) with a sharp peak at 1 ms and another at 11.7 ms. Are they wave I and a late wave? What would you do?

Answer

Probably neither: these are the two latencies the authors attributed to the device's periodic activity locked to the clicks with the fixed SOA (README, lines 142 to 144). Adding polarities does not remove them. Average the + and - clicks separately: an artifact appears at about 1 ms in one and about 11.7 ms in the other. Record new runs with the random SOA, and a Cond A run to check that the device leaves nothing locked to the triggers.

Absolute latency

Your wave V is at 6.2 ms after the trigger rows at 16 kSPS. Can you compare it with a published norm?

Answer

Not yet. The trigger time is when the audio DMA reads the click; the codec, amplifier and earphone latency, the sound's travel to the eardrum and the ADS1299 filter delay (about 0.09 ms at 16 kSPS by our calculation) all come on top, and only the last one is known. Measure the rest once, as the README suggests (the sound output through a divider into a channel, plus the acoustic path), with no participant connected, and subtract it. Within your own study, with one setup, comparisons between conditions are valid without it.
