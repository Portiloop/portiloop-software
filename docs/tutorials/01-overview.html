Tutorial 01 · Running a night

Closed-loop stimulation with the Portiloop

The Portiloop records the EEG of a sleeping participant, recognizes slow oscillations as they happen, and plays a short sound on their up-state. This page explains the idea behind closed-loop auditory stimulation (CLAS), what the device actually computes during the night, what Cond A and Cond B mean, and what a study night looks like, using a real night recorded by the lab.

What the Portiloop is

The Portiloop is an open-source, portable device for closed-loop stimulation, developed by the MIST Lab (Polytechnique Montréal) and the CL:ASP lab (Concordia University). Its paper describes it as follows:

"In this article, we propose the Portiloop, a deep learning-based, portable and low-cost closed-loop stimulation system able to target specific brain oscillations."

Valenchon et al. (2022), PLOS ONE, Abstract [1]

In practice, for the CLAS protocol of these tutorials, a Portiloop is three things in one small box:

  • An EEG amplifier. An ADS1299 chip records 8 channels (EEG, EOG, EMG and ECG in this protocol), at 250 samples per second. Electrodes plug into a single connector (Tutorial 02).
  • A small computer that analyses the signal in real time. These tutorials document the Portiloop V2.3, which the README describes as "a Google Coral Dev Board Mini and a Portiloop board" ("hardware_version": 2 in the metadata of the tutorial night). The computer filters one EEG channel (Cz) sample by sample and runs a detector on it.
  • A sound player. When the detector fires, the device plays a short sound in earphones.

You control it from a phone, over the device's own Wi-Fi network, with a web page called the Control Center (Tutorial 03). Everything is written to files (on the SD card when one is inserted, otherwise in the device's internal memory): the raw signals of the 8 channels, a column marking every detection and every sound, the settings and the last impedance check (Tutorial 05).

Note: the same device can do other thingsThe Control Center offers several "pipelines": "Acquisition only" (recording, no detection), "Sleep spindles" (a neural-network spindle detector), "Sleep slow oscillations" and "Sleep slow oscillations (upstate)" (custom_pipelines.py, lines 9 to 34). The CLAS protocol uses only "Sleep slow oscillations (upstate)", and these tutorials describe only that one.

Closed-loop auditory stimulation (CLAS)

Slow oscillations, down-states and up-states

During deep non-REM sleep, the scalp EEG is dominated by large, slow waves. At the cellular level, they reflect the slow oscillation of cortical neurons, first described in intracellular recordings by Steriade and colleagues [2]. Massimini et al. summarize it in two sentences:

"The slow oscillation comprises a hyperpolarization phase or down state, during which virtually all cortical neurons are deeply hyperpolarized and remain silent for a few hundred milliseconds. The down state is followed by a depolarization phase or up state that also lasts for several hundred milliseconds."

Massimini et al. (2004), Journal of Neuroscience, Introduction [3]

On the scalp, with a reference near the ear (as in this protocol), the down-state is associated with the large negative half-wave of the slow oscillation and the up-state with the positive half-wave that follows. The same paper is careful about the exact timing:

"Several considerations suggest that the negative peak of the slow oscillation in the ear-referenced human sleep EEG is likely to reflect the transition to the depolarizing phase or up state of the intracellularly defined slow oscillation." [...] "As shown by Steriade et al. (1994), the emergence of scalp positivity corresponds to the depolarized phase of the slow oscillation."

Massimini et al. (2004), Journal of Neuroscience, Discussion [3]

Why a sound at the up-state?

A short, soft sound during deep sleep can evoke a response in the EEG without waking the sleeper. The idea of CLAS is to time such sounds to the ongoing rhythm. The study that established the paradigm in humans compared sounds in phase and out of phase with the slow oscillation:

"Here we show in sleeping humans that auditory stimulation in phase with the ongoing rhythmic occurrence of slow oscillation up states profoundly enhances the slow oscillation rhythm, phase-coupled spindle activity, and, consequently, the consolidation of declarative memory. Stimulation out of phase with the ongoing slow oscillation rhythm remained ineffective."

Ngo et al. (2013), Neuron, Summary [4]

What is known, and what is not

The effect on the EEG is well established; the effect on memory is less certain. A double-blind replication of the word-pair experiment starts from the first and questions the second:

"Closed-loop acoustic stimulation has previously been shown to enhance slow oscillations and spindle activity during sleep and improve verbal associative memory." [...] "Again, we successfully enhanced slow oscillation and spindle power, but again did not strengthen associative memory performance with stimulation."

Henin et al. (2019), eNeuro, Abstract [5]

Two meta-analyses reached different conclusions from overlapping sets of studies:

"Acoustic enhancement of SWS tends to increase the overnight consolidation of episodic memory but effects remain small and-with the exception of subgroup models-at trend levels. Currently, the evidence is not sufficient to recommend the use of commercially available devices."

Wunderlin et al. (2021), Sleep, Abstract (10 studies, 177 participants; combined Hedges' g = 0.25, p = 0.07) [6]

"We found a medium significant effect of acoustic stimulation on memory task performance (g = 0.68, p = .031) in young adults <35 years of age, but no statistically significant effect in adults >35 years of age (g = -0.83, p = .223)." [...] "the small number of studies in this area, their small sample sizes, the short-term nature of most investigations and the high between-studies heterogeneity highlight a need for high-powered and long-term experiments"

Stanyer et al. (2022), Journal of Sleep Research, Abstract (16 studies) [7]

So, in short: CLAS reliably changes the sleeping EEG; whether, for whom and how much it changes memory is still an open question. This is exactly why studies compare stimulation nights with sham nights (Cond B and Cond A below), and why blinding matters.

For neuroscientists: what changes from one CLAS study to the nextPublished CLAS studies differ in how they detect slow waves (fixed or adaptive thresholds, which channel, which filters), which phase they target, the sound, its loudness, how many sounds are played in a row, and which sleep stages are targeted. The Portiloop protocol fixes these choices in code. The next section lists them, so you can compare them with the study you want to replicate. The source of the detection thresholds used by the Portiloop is not documented in the code; we do not know which publication they come from.

What the Portiloop computes during the night

The CLAS protocol selects the pipeline "Sleep slow oscillations (upstate)", the detection channel 6 (Cz) and a sampling rate of 250 Hz (Tutorial 03). The pipeline has three parts: a filter, a detector and a stimulator (SlowOscillationFilter, SOUpstateDetector and SlowOscillationStimulator in custom_pipelines.py). A new sample of Cz arrives every 4 ms and goes through all three.

1. The filters

The device cannot use the zero-phase filters of offline analysis, because they need future samples. It uses causal filters, which only use the present and past samples, in this order (custom_processors.py, SlowOscillationFilter):

            low = 0.5
            high = 30.0
            fir_coef = firwin(20, [low, high], pass_zero=False, window="hamming", fs=sampling_rate)
        ...
        alpha = 0.005
        ...
        so_fir_coeffs = firwin(17, [0.16, 4], fs=sampling_rate, pass_zero="bandpass")
        ...
        self.filter_parts = [fir, notch, dc, so_fir]
  1. a 20-coefficient FIR band-pass, nominally 0.5 to 30 Hz;
  2. a notch at the mains frequency (the "Notch filter frequency" setting: 50 Hz in the protocol, 60 Hz in the tutorial night);
  3. a DC removal: the signal minus its exponential running average (alpha = 0.005);
  4. a 17-coefficient FIR band-pass, nominally 0.16 to 4 Hz.

Two consequences matter for the experimenter. First, the two FIR filters delay the signal by half their length: 9.5 + 8 samples, that is 70 ms at 250 Hz (the DC removal and the notch add small phase shifts at low frequencies). The detector therefore always works on a slightly late copy of the EEG, and the up-state timing below is measured on that copy: it predicts the peak of the filtered signal, not of the EEG itself. Second, filters this short cannot cut sharply at 4 Hz. We computed the response of the whole chain from its coefficients (our computation, not a statement of the code): it passes 0.19 to 10.4 Hz at half power (−3 dB), and still lets through 75% of the amplitude at 10 Hz and 58% at 13 Hz. The detector therefore sees slow waves with alpha and some spindle activity on top of them; you will see this in the widget below.

2. The slow-wave criteria

The detector cuts the filtered signal into waves: each wave starts when the signal crosses zero going down, so it starts with its negative half-wave (the down-state) and continues with its positive half-wave (the up-state). For the current wave, it keeps the maximum, the minimum and the time spent below and above zero, and checks four fixed criteria (custom_detectors.py):

        self.th_PaP = 75  # total amplitude threshold
        self.th_Neg = 40  # negative amplitude threshold
        self.min_tNe = 125  # minimum duration spent below 0 (in ms)
        self.max_tNe = 1500  # maximum duration spent below 0 (in ms)
        self.max_tPo = 1000  # maximum duration spent above 0 (in ms)
        ...
        so_detected = (
            tp2p > self.th_PaP  # peak-to-peak > amplitude threshold
            and tneg > self.th_Neg  # amplitude portion below 0 > negative amplitude threshold
            and self.min_tNe < tne < self.max_tNe  # the duration spent below 0 is within range
            and tpo < self.max_tPo  # duration spent above zero < maximum duration above zero
        )

These values are in microvolts of the filtered signal, they are the same for every participant (not adaptive), and they are not shown or editable in the Control Center. A participant whose slow waves are smaller on this montage will simply receive fewer sounds.

3. Timing the sound to the up-state

With the "(upstate)" pipeline, meeting the criteria is not enough: the detector waits for the moment when the positive peak is expected. When the signal crosses zero going up, it starts counting samples. At the end of every wave that met the criteria, it measures how long it took from that upward crossing to the positive peak and updates a running average of this delay:

        so_upstate_detected = (
            so_detected
            and self.est_t_upstate is not None
            and self.counter_upstate is not None
            and self.counter_upstate == self.est_t_upstate
        )
        ...
                        # compute a running average
                        self.est_t_upstate = int(self.alpha * self.t_upstate + (1 - self.alpha) * self.est_t_upstate)

This is the code that recorded the tutorial night (commit 917f1f3, custom_detectors.py, lines 250 to 267), which recorded the tutorial night: a copy of this detector, run on the night, fires on exactly the 1,930 samples where the device played a sound (our check).

With alpha = 0.1, each new wave moves the estimate by 10% of the difference, so the estimate follows the participant's own slow waves, mostly the last 10 to 20 waves that met the criteria. The detection fires on the single sample where the count equals the estimate, if the wave still meets the criteria. Four details follow from the code:

  • The first good wave after each Start makes no sound. There is no estimate yet; that wave only provides the first one.
  • At most one sound per wave. The stimulator also refuses a sound less than 0.1 s after the previous one (self.wait_t = .1 in custom_stimulators.py). With the protocol's delays all at 0 ms, nothing else spaces the sounds: consecutive slow waves can each receive one.
  • On the tutorial night, the estimate had a median of 184 ms after the upward zero crossing (5th to 95th percentile: 88 to 292 ms) at the 1,930 detections (our computation, with a copy of this detector).
Known issue, fixed on 2026-11-26: the sounds of the tutorial night came slightly earlyThe version that recorded the tutorial night (commit 917f1f3, the code quoted on this page) truncated the estimate to a whole number of samples at each update (int(...)), so it settled 4 to 5 samples below the true average delay and the stimulations came about 16 to 20 ms early. This was fixed on main on 2026-11-26 (commit e4959f1), which keeps the estimate as a float and compares the count with round(...): devices updated after that date stimulate later by that amount. All the numbers of these tutorials come from the tutorial night, with the truncation. Note the software version (commit) of each study night, so that nights recorded before and after the fix are not mixed unknowingly.

The sound itself is the file 15msPN_48kHz_norm_stereo.wav ("detection_sound" in portiloop/src/core/constants.py): a 15 ms stereo noise burst at 48 kHz. "PN" in the name suggests pink noise, but in our check of the file the energy per octave rises by about 3 dB per octave, which is the signature of white noise (pink noise has the same energy in every octave); we do not know which was intended. The "Sound output volume" is a software percentage, not a calibrated sound level: the sound pressure in the ear is not known unless you measure it with your earphones.

Over the whole night, the sounds fall near the positive peak of the slow waves. Treat this as approximate: a zero-phase filter also "sees" the response evoked by the sound, so it cannot give the phase of the ongoing wave alone. The average response around the sounds (Tutorial 07) tells the same story: a deep minimum 0.42 s before the sound (the detected down-state), and the maximum of the slow band 0.18 s after it.

Blinding in practice

The protocol only says: "In the Control panel, select Cond A or Cond B, depending on the experimenter's instructions." The software does not blind anybody:

  • the meaning of A and B is fixed in the code and published (on this page, among others), so "A" and "B" are not a secret code;
  • the file name (CONDA/CONDB) and the metadata ("stimulate") state the condition of every recording;
  • the toggle keeps its last position after a restart (it is saved with the other settings), so the next person who opens the Control Center sees the condition of the previous night.

Some things do help: "Test Sound" plays the sound in both conditions, so the setup is the same for the participant; and the Portiloop's indicator LED, which the code would set to a different colour for stimulation and sham, is disabled by default according to the README ("disabled by default, can be enabled programatically"; not verified on the hardware). The participant may still hear sounds during the night in Cond B, which is in the nature of the paradigm.

Blinding is therefore organizational. Decide, before the study, who knows the condition of each night (the person who prepares the schedule), who sets the toggle, who scores and analyses the data, and how the analysis is kept blind (for example, by renaming the files before analysis). Tutorial 09 discusses this for the analysis.

Old recordings: check the software versionOn 2026-10-13, one commit (fad9e20) mapped the toggle the other way round (Cond A was stimulation); a commit of the same day (b041e3c) set the current mapping. If you analyse recordings from that period, rely on "stimulate" in _metadata.json, not on the A/B letters.

What a study night looks like

  1. Preparation (before bed). Charge and check the material, connect the electrodes, place them, check the impedances (Tutorials 02 and 03). Set the settings of the protocol, the condition and the recording name, put the earphones in and test the sound.
  2. Start, then a delay without sound. When the participant is ready to sleep, press Start. The setting "Stimulation starts after" pauses detection and stimulation for that many minutes. The protocol says: "This duration corresponds to the period during which no sound is played at the beginning of the recording. It can be adjusted based on the expected time needed to fall asleep." (its example is 45 minutes). The recording itself starts immediately.
  3. The night. The device detects slow waves and plays sounds (Cond B) or only logs them (Cond A). If an electrode falls off, the lead-off protection may pause detection (Tutorial 04). If the participant wakes up and the sounds keep them awake, the protocol says: "press the Stop button, then immediately press the Start button. This will give you another X minutes without any sounds, where X is the duration you set in Step 18." Each Start creates a new file.
  4. The morning. The protocol: "On the phone, in the Portiloop Control Center, press the Stop button", then turn off the Portiloop ("only one short press"), unplug the earphones and remove the SD card. Note that a recording also stops by itself after 10 hours ("duration": 36000 seconds in constants.py).

The tutorial night

All the widgets of these tutorials use one real night, recorded by the lab as a test of the protocol with the "Sleep slow oscillations (upstate)" pipeline in Cond B (the file name ends with CONDB). Its settings, read from its _metadata.json: 250 Hz, detection channel 6 (Cz), volume 50, "Stimulation starts after" 25 minutes, all stimulation delays at 0, and a notch at 60 Hz. The protocol asks for 50 Hz, the mains frequency where the study runs; this test night used 60 Hz, so do not copy that setting.

  • Duration: 7.39 h (443.6 minutes). Sounds were possible (stimulation_on = 1) from minute 25.0, when the delay ended, to minute 373.4.
  • 1,930 sounds, from minute 30.9 to minute 373.0. The median interval between consecutive sounds is 3.10 s; the shortest is 0.328 s.
  • Around minute 373 a movement disturbed every channel and the Cz electrode (the detection electrode) came off: its raw signal stayed at the limit of the amplifier (±187,500 µV) for most of the remaining 70 minutes, while the other channels kept recording. Detection stopped at minute 373.4 because of the lead-off protection, and the recording went on, without sounds, until it was stopped at 7.39 h.

Exercises

Why did these slow waves get no sound?

In the closed-loop widget, select sound 4 (at 12.2 s) with "what the detector saw" turned on, and look at the large slow wave just before it, with its trough near 11.3 s. Then select sound 10 (at 23.6 s) and look at the small wave just after it, near 23.9 s. Using the four criteria and the up-state timing, explain why the device stayed silent on these two waves.

Answer

Look at the detector's input, not at the display trace. A wave gets a sound only if, at the sample where the count since the upward zero crossing equals the estimate (200 to 264 ms in this excerpt), the wave has a peak-to-peak amplitude above 75 µV, went below −40 µV, stayed below zero between 125 and 1500 ms, and has not stayed above zero for 1000 ms. We checked both waves with the copy of the detector in docs/tools/build_01-overview_data.py. The wave near 11.3 s is deep (the detector's input reaches −106 µV), but its input crosses zero going up at 11.488 s and goes back below zero 32 ms later, at 11.520 s, long before the estimated delay: the downward crossing ends the wave and resets the count, so the sample at which the sound would have been played never comes. The next wave, which starts at that crossing, meets the criteria and gets sound 4. The small wave near 23.9 s only reaches −29 µV at the detector's input, which fails the −40 µV criterion. In general, faster activity riding on a slow wave can cut it into short pieces in this way, because the online filters let activity up to about 10 Hz through.

What does the sham night contain?

A colleague says: "In Cond A the Portiloop does nothing, so the sham file is just an EEG recording." What is wrong with this statement, and why does it matter for the analysis?

Answer

In Cond A, detection runs exactly as in Cond B and the stimulation column marks every moment a sound would have been played; only the sound is not played. This gives the analysis matched events in both conditions: you can average the EEG around the sham markers and compare with the average around real sounds, at the same phase of the same kind of slow waves. Without these markers, a sham night could only be compared with a stimulation night globally (for example, total slow-wave power).

Plan the delay

In the tutorial night, the delay was 25 minutes and the first sound came at minute 30.9. Use the night widget to estimate when the participant fell asleep. Would the protocol's example of 45 minutes have changed anything for this night? What would you set for a participant who usually needs an hour to fall asleep?

Answer

The alpha band fades after about 20 minutes, and slow-wave power appears later; the first sound, at minute 30.9, is 5.9 minutes after the end of the delay (the first good slow wave after the delay sets the timing estimate without a sound). With 45 minutes, the first quarter of an hour of sounds would not have been played: whether that matters depends on whether your study targets the first sleep cycle. The delay should cover the expected sleep latency of the participant, as the protocol says; for someone who needs an hour, a delay of about an hour avoids sounds during wake and light sleep. If the participant is still awake when the delay ends, the protocol's remedy is Stop then Start, which restarts the delay (in a new file).
