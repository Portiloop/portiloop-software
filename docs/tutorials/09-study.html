Tutorial 09 · Processing the recordings

From one night to a study

One stimulation night shows that the Portiloop detected slow oscillations and played sounds; it cannot show that the sounds did anything. This tutorial explains what a study needs on top of that: a sham condition, a design, blinding that the software does not provide, measures and statistics chosen before looking at the data, the group notebook of the analysis package, and the pitfalls that are specific to the Portiloop software.

Why one night proves little

In tutorial 07 you averaged the EEG of the tutorial night (a test night recorded by the lab in Cond B, sound played) around its 1,930 stimulation markers. With the default settings of portiloop_analysis, 1,707 epochs were kept, and the average slow-band response at Cz has a minimum of -68.0 µV at -0.42 s, a maximum of +23.7 µV at +0.18 s (where the sound lands), then a negative wave near +0.6 s (about -27 µV) and a positive wave near +1 s (about +18 µV); its P900 minus N550 amplitude is 31.0 µV.

Most of that picture is guaranteed by the way the markers were chosen, whatever the sound does:

  • Selection. A marker is placed only when the detector has just seen a large slow oscillation on Cz (peak-to-peak above 75 µV, negative part beyond 40 µV, and duration limits; see tutorial 03), and it fires at the predicted up-state. An average aligned on these moments must show a deep down-state before 0 and an up-state at 0. The same would happen with no sound at all.
  • Continuation. Slow oscillations come in trains, so a detected one is often followed by another about a second later. A negative then positive wave after the marker can therefore be partly endogenous.
  • Time of night and stage. Markers cluster in the periods with most slow-wave activity, so any measure averaged over markers describes those periods, not the night.

What would show an effect of the sound is a difference between nights that are identical except for the sound. That is what the two conditions of the Control Center are for. In Cond A the whole pipeline runs (filtering, detection, the stimulation decision, the markers in the stimulation column), but the sound is not played:

    def send_stimulation(self, lsl_text, sound):
        stim_enabled = self.config_dict.get('stimulate', True) if self.config_dict is not None else True

The condition comes from the Control tab toggle: forward=lambda x: x == 'Cond B' sets stim_on, which is written as run_dict['stimulate'] (simple_gui.py, lines 625 to 629, and line 195). So Cond B is stimulation and Cond A is sham. In both, the CSV has the same columns and the stimulation column marks the times a sound was played (Cond B) or would have been played (Cond A).

For neuroscientists: the sham condition of Ngo et al. (2013)The study that introduced closed-loop auditory stimulation of slow oscillations used the same logic [1]: "During the Sham condition, stimulation time points were marked but no stimulation was applied." Their comparison of the averages is the template for a Portiloop study: "Whereas in the Sham condition an individual SO cycle occurred, the two auditory stimuli in phase with the predicted SO up states formed a sequence of three succeeding SO cycles". The sham average also shows a slow oscillation; the effect is the difference. (Their protocol played two sounds per detection; the Portiloop upstate pipeline plays one.)
What the tutorial night can and cannot tell youThe tutorial night is a single Cond B night. It is perfect for learning the files and the analysis, and it shows that detection and targeting work (the sound lands near the up-state). It cannot show whether the waves after the marker were evoked by the sound, because there is no sham night of the same person to compare with. We do not have one, so on this page the stim versus sham comparison is illustrated with synthetic data, clearly labelled.

Study designs

Within-subject: stimulation and sham nights in each participant

Each participant sleeps one Cond B night and one Cond A night; half of the participants have the stimulation night first, half the sham night first (counterbalancing), so that order effects (better sleep on the second night, practice on the memory task) cancel out on average. This is the design of Ngo et al. (2013): "Each subject was tested in two experimental conditions, a Stimulation condition and a Sham condition, with the order of conditions balanced across subjects. The subject's two experimental conditions were separated by an interval of at least 1 week." [1]

  • Adaptation night. The first night with electrodes is usually worse (the "first-night effect"). Ngo et al. used one: "subjects were accustomed to sleeping under laboratory conditions during an adaptation night, including the attachment of electrodes" [1]. With the Portiloop, a suggestion is to run the adaptation night exactly like an experimental night in Cond A (no sound), so the participant and the experimenter practise the whole procedure; do not analyse it as a sham night.
  • Why it is efficient. People differ a lot in slow-wave amplitude (with age, skull thickness, electrode contact). In a within-subject design, each participant is their own control, and a paired test removes those differences. The simulator below shows how much this matters.
  • Behavioural outcomes need parallel versions of the task (different word lists on the two nights), assigned in counterbalanced order too.

Between groups

The second analysis notebook, group_comparison.ipynb, compares groups of participants: "here, younger and older adults". A comparison between groups uses independent (unpaired) tests, and needs more participants for the same precision because people differ. Comparing groups on stimulation nights only tells you that the response to stimulation and selection differ between groups (older adults have smaller slow waves, so the detector selects different events). If you want to know whether the effect of stimulation differs between groups, each participant needs both a stim and a sham night (a mixed design: condition within subjects, group between subjects), and the test is on the per-participant stim minus sham differences.

Blinding in practice

The protocol tells the experimenter to "select Cond A or Cond B, depending on the experimenter's instructions". Neutral labels are a good start, but the software does not blind anyone: the meaning of A and B is fixed in the code (and documented on this site), and the condition is written in several places.

Who knows what

Blinding is therefore organisational. A workable split of roles (a suggestion, to adapt to your ethics approval and team size):

Key holderGenerates the assignment and the codes, keeps the key, makes the blinded copy of the files. Does not run nights, score sleep, test participants or analyse.
Night experimenterReceives only "participant 07, night 2: Cond A". Is not told what A and B mean (they can find out, so count them as not blind and keep them away from the outcome measures).
Morning tester and sleep scorerRun the memory tests and score the sleep without knowing the condition.
AnalystWorks on coded files, with the pairing of nights but not the condition, until the analysis script is frozen.

A suggested procedure

  1. Before the first participant: write the analysis plan (measures, windows, tests, exclusion rules; see below) and register it.
  2. The key holder generates the assignment with the helper below (or any randomisation tool) and keeps the key file away from the shared folders.
  3. Each night, the experimenter sets the toggle as written on the night sheet, presses Start, and checks that the Current experiment label ends with the expected suffix. (Seeing the suffix is unavoidable; it is the only check that the right condition is running.)
  4. In the morning, the SD card goes to the key holder, who copies the folders and logs which folder belongs to which participant and night.
  5. The key holder makes the blinded copy: every file renamed with the night's code, the date and time kept (needed to put several files of one night in order), and the fields "stimulate" and "filename" removed from the metadata. The script below does it; logs and Previous run.json are not passed on.
  6. The analyst develops and freezes the analysis on the coded files: rejection rules, number of kept epochs per night, exclusions, the exact script. Only then does the key holder reveal which code is stim, and the frozen script is run once.
"""Make a blinded copy of Portiloop recordings for the analysts. Run by the key holder only.

rename_map.csv has two columns, folder and code: one row per recording folder (a night interrupted by Stop and
Start has several folders, which all get the code of that night).
"""
import csv
import json
import re
import shutil
from pathlib import Path

SOURCE = Path("recordings")    # the folders copied from the SD cards (names end with _CONDA or _CONDB)
TARGET = Path("blinded")       # the copy given to the analysts
HIDDEN = ("stimulate", "filename")  # metadata fields that reveal the condition
NAME = re.compile(r"^.*_(\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2}_\d{2}-\d{2}-\d{2})_COND[AB](.*)$")

with open("rename_map.csv", newline="") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        folder, code = SOURCE / row["folder"], row["code"]
        stamp = NAME.match(folder.name).group(1)
        out = TARGET / f"{code}_{stamp}"     # keep the date and time: load_recording orders files with it
        out.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=False)
        for file in sorted(folder.iterdir()):
            match = NAME.match(file.name)
            if match is None:
                print("not copied (unexpected name):", file)
                continue
            new = out / f"{code}_{match.group(1)}{match.group(2)}"
            if file.name.endswith("_metadata.json"):
                metadata = json.loads(file.read_text(encoding="utf-8-sig"))
                for field in HIDDEN:
                    metadata.pop(field, None)
                new.write_text(json.dumps(metadata, indent=4))
            else:
                shutil.copy2(file, new)
            print(file.name, "->", new.name)

We tested it on synthetic recordings named like real ones (one Cond B night, and a Cond A night split in two files by a Stop/Start): the copy contained no COND and no stimulate, and load_recording read the two files of the coded sham night in the right order, with the gap between them. The script keeps the date and time in the names, so the analyst knows which night came first; that does not reveal the condition as long as the order of conditions is random per participant and stays with the key holder.

The data can unblindCoding the files hides the labels, not the physiology. A clear evoked N550/P900 in the average of one night tells the analyst which night was stimulated. This is why the plan must be fixed before the analyst sees any average: blinding then protects the choices that cannot be fully specified in advance (rejection of bad nights, sleep scoring), and the registered plan protects the rest.

Prefer measures based on fixed windows (window means) to measures that search for a peak: the maximum of a noisy average grows with the noise, so a night with fewer epochs would look like it had a larger peak. A window mean has no such bias.

Choosing windows and statistics before looking

A response averaged over thousands of epochs has a hundred or more time points, two bands and several electrodes. If you look at the stim and sham averages, see where they differ most, and then test that window, the test will be "significant" far too often: the selection and the test use the same noise. Kriegeskorte et al. (2009) call this circular analysis, or "double dipping" [3]. The cure is to write down, before the data exist (or before anyone unblinded sees them), the outcome, its window, the electrode, the test, and the exclusion rules, and to register that plan [4]. Anything decided later is reported as exploratory.

The unit of analysis is the participant

Compute one average per participant and night, then do statistics on those averages (n = number of participants). Do not pool the epochs of all participants into one big set and test epochs: epochs from the same night are not independent (the tutorial night has a median interval of 3.10 s between stimulations, 5 % of the intervals are shorter than 0.81 s, so 10 s epochs overlap heavily), and the question is about people, not about slow oscillations. A test on pooled epochs has thousands of degrees of freedom and will find tiny, meaningless differences. The sem method of the package says the same about a single night: "Epochs closer than their length share samples, so the SEM may underestimate the uncertainty."

Paired tests for within-subject designs

For stim and sham nights of the same people, use a paired test on the per-participant values: it tests whether the stim minus sham differences are centred on zero, and removes the differences between people. compare_time_courses in portiloop_analysis.measures does this time point by time point when called with paired=True ("True when the rows of a and b are the same participants in the same order (within-subject design, e.g. a stimulation night and a sham night per participant): paired t-tests"); it then calls stats.ttest_rel and corrects with the FDR. Its default, paired=False, runs independent t-tests (stats.ttest_ind), which is right only for two groups of different people. The rows of the two arrays must be in the same participant order; the function checks only that the shapes match. For the single pre-chosen measure, a paired t-test from scipy is enough:

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from portiloop_analysis import (EpochSettings, compare_time_courses, event_locked_responses,
                                evoked_slow_wave_amplitude, load_recording)

settings = EpochSettings(notch_hz=50)  # the mains frequency where the nights were recorded
nights = {  # participant: (files of the stimulation night, files of the sham night), filled after unblinding
    "P01": (["blinded/K7QX_2026-11-02_22-01-03/K7QX_2026-11-02_22-01-03.csv"],
            ["blinded/R2MD_2026-11-09_22-05-41/R2MD_2026-11-09_22-05-41.csv",
             "blinded/R2MD_2026-11-09_23-40-12/R2MD_2026-11-09_23-40-12.csv"]),
    # ... one line per participant
}

stim, sham, kept = [], [], []
for participant, (stim_files, sham_files) in nights.items():
    pair = []
    for files, averages in ((stim_files, stim), (sham_files, sham)):
        responses = event_locked_responses(load_recording(files, channel="Cz", verbose=False), settings)
        averages.append(responses.mean("slow"))  # one average per participant and night
        pair.append(responses.n_kept)
    kept.append((participant, *pair))
time = responses.time
stim, sham = np.array(stim), np.array(sham)
print("kept epochs (participant, stim, sham):", kept)

# 1. The main measure: P900 minus N550, one number per participant and night, paired t-test
amp_stim, amp_sham = evoked_slow_wave_amplitude(time, stim), evoked_slow_wave_amplitude(time, sham)
diff = amp_stim - amp_sham
test = stats.ttest_rel(amp_stim, amp_sham)
n = len(diff)
half = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1) * diff.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
print(f"P900-N550, stim minus sham: {diff.mean():.1f} uV, 95% CI {diff.mean() - half:.1f} to "
      f"{diff.mean() + half:.1f}, t({n - 1}) = {test.statistic:.2f}, p = {test.pvalue:.3g}, "
      f"dz = {diff.mean() / diff.std(ddof=1):.2f}")

# 2. The time course: paired t-test at every time point of the window chosen in advance, then FDR
p, p_fdr = compare_time_courses(stim, sham, time, window=(0.4, 1.1), paired=True)
print("time points with FDR-corrected p < 0.05:", time[p_fdr < 0.05])

For a comparison between groups, the same function with its default (independent t-tests): p, p_fdr = compare_time_courses(young, older, time, window=(0.4, 1.1)), with one average per participant in each array (the groups may have different sizes). Its docstring gives the rule: "Testing fewer time points makes the correction less severe; choose the window before looking at the data."

Multiple time points and the FDR

Testing every time point at p < 0.05 almost guarantees false positives somewhere: an epoch of -5 to +5 s at 250 Hz has 2,501 time points. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [2] (fdr_bh) controls the false discovery rate: among the time points declared significant, the expected proportion of false ones stays below 5 %. It sorts the p-values, multiplies the k-th smallest by m/k (m = number of tests), and makes the result monotonic. Two consequences:

  • The fewer tests, the milder the correction. A window chosen in advance (for example 0.4 to 1.1 s, around the N550 and P900) gives more power where you expect the effect than testing the whole epoch.
  • The strongest design is a single number per participant chosen in advance (the P900 minus N550 amplitude): one test, no correction. The time-point tests then describe when the conditions differ, as a secondary analysis.

Cluster-based permutation tests [5] are a common alternative for EEG time courses; they are not in portiloop_analysis.

Effect sizes and confidence intervals

Report the mean stim minus sham difference in µV with its 95 % confidence interval, and a standardized effect size. For within-subject designs this is Cohen's dz: in Lakens' words, "The standardized mean difference effect size for within-subjects designs is referred to as Cohen's dz", with "the standard deviation of the difference scores" as denominator [6]. The same paper recommends to "report the confidence interval around the effect size". A p-value alone says nothing about how large the effect is, and a non-significant result with a wide interval is not evidence of no effect.

That first draw is a typical small study. The true effect is there (it is built in), yet the P900 minus N550 difference is 9.5 µV with a 95 % confidence interval from -0.5 to 19.4 µV (paired p = 0.060, dz = 0.60): the data are compatible with no effect and with a large one. The group_comparison notebook

Adapting it to stim versus sham

The demo recordings are all stimulation nights (their names end with _CONDB, like the device's stimulation recordings). For a within-subject study, list two rows per participant (one per night) and use the condition column (described in the notebook as "any other grouping you want to keep in the table") for "stim" and "sham"; then compare per-participant averages with the paired code above, not with plot_groups and independent tests.

Values at t = 0 include the response after itThe filters of portiloop_analysis are zero-phase (they run forwards and backwards), so they spread a response in both directions in time. We checked this with the notebook's own settings (0.5 to 4 Hz, baseline -2.5 to -0.5 s) on a signal containing only the evoked wave of the demo recordings (60 µV, which is exactly 0 µV at t = 0): after filtering, the value at t = 0 is +6.1 µV (and +3.9 µV at -0.25 s). The demo participants' slow-band values at t = 0 (0.6 to 5.7 µV, larger for the "young" group) come from this leakage, since the synthetic stimulations are not locked to any phase. With real data, a stim versus sham or group difference at t = 0 can therefore reflect the response after the sound, not only the state that was targeted. The notebook now warns about it in its section 5: "Differences between groups at t = 0 can therefore come from the response that follows the sound."

Pitfalls

Different numbers of stimulations between conditions

The two nights of a participant rarely have the same number of markers: sleep differs from night to night, and the sound itself may change how many slow oscillations qualify (Ngo et al. describe stimulation producing trains of slow oscillations [1]). With all delays at 0, the only spacing the software imposes between sounds is wait_t = .1 s, so nearby detections are stimulated too (minimum interval on the tutorial night: 0.328 s). Consequences: a night with fewer epochs has a noisier average; epochs with a neighbouring stimulation inside them contain part of its response; and the composition of the epochs (stage, time of night) can differ. Report the counts per condition, test them as an outcome, use window means rather than peaks, and consider the min_interval_s setting or the same time range in both nights. On the tutorial night, the first half contributed 974 kept epochs and the second half 733.

Stop and Start during the night

When a participant presses Stop then Start (as the protocol allows), the software starts a new recording: a new file and folder with a new date and time, a short gap, and the "Stimulation starts after" delay counted again from the new Start, with detection paused in both conditions. A night may therefore consist of several files and have less stimulation time. Load all files of a night together (load_recording([file1, file2])), which places them on one timeline with the gap as missing data; epochs touching the gap are rejected. Check that every file of a night has the same condition suffix and "stimulate" value: the toggle is only locked during a recording, so it can be changed between Stop and Start.

Nights with lead-off

If the detection electrode is disconnected, or the reference or bias comes off, detection and stimulation pause (for the rest of the recording when the detection channel is disabled). The tutorial night shows the result: the Cz electrode (the detection electrode) came off around minute 373 and the last 70 minutes have no stimulation. Keep the _leadoff.json file with each recording (load_recording uses it to mark the data as missing; the tutorial night's file was not retrieved), and define in the plan when a night is excluded (for example a minimum number of kept epochs, or of minutes with detection running), applied blind to the condition.

The condition is remembered

The toggle state is saved and restored: "stim_on" is one of the PERSISTED_FIELDS of the Control Center, and so is "custom_exp_name", the recording name. A new session opens with the last condition and the last participant's name. Loading a preset also restores the condition saved in it. Every night, set the toggle and the recording name actively, then read the Current experiment label after Start.

Known issue: recordings with the opposite meaning of Cond A and Cond B

Commit fad9e20 (2026-10-13, 03:58, "Changed names of STIM ON and STIM OFF to COND B and COND A") mapped the names the other way round:

-        stim_str = "STIMON" if self.stim_on else "STIMOFF"
+        stim_str = "CONDA" if self.stim_on else "CONDB"
...
+                        forward=lambda x: x == 'Cond A',

Commit b041e3c the same day (06:45) set the current mapping ("CONDB" if self.stim_on else "CONDA"). A recording made on a device running fad9e20 or the commit between them (0b699e2) has the opposite meaning: _CONDA was the stimulation night. Two checks:

  • Trust "stimulate" in _metadata.json, not the suffix: in all these versions it is run_dict['stimulate'] = self.stim_on, true when the sound was played.
  • The software version does not help ("0.2.0" before and after), but b041e3c also added "pipeline" and "sleep_timeout" to the metadata. A _CONDA/_CONDB file whose metadata has no "sleep_timeout" was made with the swapped mapping.

Related, also on 2026-10-13: before commit 9d598ac (07:12), the stimulator was created only when stimulating (create_stimulator = config_dict['detect'] and config_dict['stimulate'] and stimulator_cls is not None), so sham recordings made with earlier versions have no stimulation column. Their detection column still marks the detections, and load_recording(files, markers="detection") uses it as the event times (the default, markers="stimulation", stops with an error that points to this option). On the tutorial night (Cond B) the detection and stimulation columns have the same 1,930 markers, but we have not checked whether they always coincide (the stimulator's 0.1 s minimum gap, for example, applies to sounds, not to detections); the function's docstring says the same: "Check that detections and stimulations coincide in your version of the software before relying on "detection"." If the stim nights are loaded with their stimulation column and the old sham nights with detection, the two conditions are not marked in exactly the same way: say so in the report.

Stage selection from automatic staging

Keeping only N2 and N3 stimulations (stages=("N2", "N3")) is only as good as the hypnogram. The automatic staging used offline in tutorial 06 has not been validated on Portiloop recordings, because no expert-scored Portiloop data was available. On the tutorial night it scored no N3 at all, and labelled some stimulation-rich periods (minutes 235 to 280) as wake: of the 1,930 stimulations, 1,157 fall in its N2 epochs and 683 in its W epochs. Selecting N2/N3 with it would keep at most the 1,103 kept epochs in its N2 and silently drop 544 kept epochs it called wake. The errors may also differ between conditions (sounds evoke K-complex-like waves that a stager may read differently; we have not tested this). Use expert scoring done blind to the condition, or at least check the automatic stages (tutorial 06) and report results with all stimulations as well.

Different settings between nights

The two nights must differ only in the condition. Compare the _metadata.json files of each pair: pipeline, notch (the tutorial night was recorded with 60 Hz although the protocol asks for 50 Hz), volume, "Stimulation starts after" (sleep_timeout), detection channel, and the delays. Several of these are remembered from the previous session, so a change made for one participant carries over to the next.

Exercises

How often do uncorrected tests find something when there is nothing?

In the simulator, set the true effect to 0 (12 participants, variability 10 µV) and click "Repeat 1,000 studies". In what fraction of the studies does at least one time point reach p < 0.05 without correction, with FDR over all points, and with FDR in the window?

Answer

With these settings (the repetitions use a fixed seed, so you get the same numbers as we did): 88.4 % of the studies have at least one "significant" time point without correction, 5.1 % with FDR over the 101 points, and 4.5 % with FDR over the 18 points of the window. With no true effect, every one of these is a false positive. FDR keeps the rate near 5 % here because, when no effect exists anywhere, every discovery is false, and controlling the false discovery rate at 5 % then amounts to controlling the chance of any false positive at 5 %.

When does pairing help?

Set the effect to 8 µV and compare the power (fraction of studies with p < 0.05 on P900 minus N550) of the paired and the independent test, with a variability of 0, 10 and 20 µV.

Answer

In our runs: with variability 0, paired 62 % and independent 67 % (with no stable differences between people, pairing brings nothing and halves the degrees of freedom); with 10 µV, paired 53 % and independent 11 %; with 20 µV, paired 39 % and independent 0.6 %. Real participants differ a lot, which is why within-subject designs with paired tests are the norm in CLAS studies. Note also that the time-point tests (any significant point with FDR in the window: 26.5 % at 10 µV) have less power than the single pre-chosen measure.

What must the key holder hide?

An analyst receives a USB stick with the SD card folders, the device log and the Previous run.json preset. List everything on it that reveals the condition.

Answer

The folder and file names (_CONDA/_CONDB), the "stimulate" and "filename" fields of every _metadata.json, the STIMON = True/False lines of portiloop.log, and "stim_on" in Previous run.json. Only the coded copy (renamed files, two fields removed) should be given. The stimulation and stimulation_on columns do not reveal it; the averages themselves may.

Interrupted sham night

A participant pressed Stop and Start at 02:10 during a Cond A night with "Stimulation starts after" set to 45 minutes. What will you find on the SD card, and what does it mean for the comparison with the stim night?

Answer

Two folders with two date-time stamps, both ending in _CONDA (check it, and check "stimulate": false in both metadata files). After 02:10 there are no markers for 45 minutes (detection paused), and a short gap between the files. Load both files together; compare the number of markers and minutes of detection with the stim night, and apply the exclusion rule of your plan without looking at which night is which.

Note: how the widget's statistics were checked

The simulator's code (its random generator, model and tests) was run outside the browser with Node.js on the default example, and the 12 × 2 synthetic averages were passed to Python: scipy.stats.ttest_rel at each time point, portiloop_analysis.measures.fdr_bh on all points and on the window, evoked_slow_wave_amplitude on the same time axis, scipy.stats.ttest_ind and scipy.stats.t.ppf for the interval. The largest relative difference in any p-value was below 10-13, and the confidence limits differed by less than 10-10 µV. Separately, the t-tests, the quantile function and the FDR were compared with scipy on 24 random data sets of 3 to 40 participants (p-values down to 10-18): relative errors below 10-13 for the p-values, identical FDR values, and differences below 10-10 for the t quantiles and the confidence limits. The scripts are kept with the site's working files, not on the site.
